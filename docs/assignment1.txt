
Automatic Text Summarization with Hybrid Extractive-Abstractive Methods: A Literature Review

1. Introduction
The exponential growth of digital information across various domains, including scientific literature, news articles, and social media, has necessitated efficient methods for information distillation. Automatic Text Summarization (ATS) stands as a critical Natural Language Processing (NLP) task aimed at condensing lengthy texts into shorter, coherent summaries while preserving their core meaning. This process significantly reduces the effort required for human readers to grasp the essential content of vast document collections.

Traditionally, ATS techniques are broadly categorized into two main paradigms: extractive and abstractive summarization. Extractive summarization identifies and selects the most important sentences or phrases directly from the source document to form a summary. While this approach ensures factual accuracy and grammatical correctness, it often lacks coherence and fluency, as the extracted sentences may not flow naturally together. Conversely, abstractive summarization generates new sentences and phrases that convey the essential information of the source text, mimicking human-like summarization. This method offers greater flexibility in linguistic expressions and can produce more concise and fluent summaries. However, abstractive models are prone to challenges such as factual inaccuracies, hallucinations, and grammatical errors, particularly when dealing with complex or lengthy documents.

To leverage the strengths of both approaches and mitigate their individual weaknesses, hybrid extractive-abstractive summarization methods have emerged as a promising research direction. These hybrid systems typically involve a multi-stage process where an extractive component first identifies salient information, which is then refined or rewritten by an abstract component.

This literature review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the advancements in automatic text summarization, with a specific focus on hybrid extractive-abstractive methods. The review defines the objectives and scope, organizes relevant papers by themes and methodologies, critically analyzes past work, identifies research gaps, and highlights future directions in this evolving field. The scope of this review primarily covers research papers published within the last five years, focusing on high-quality, peer-reviewed sources from reputable academic databases such as IEEE, ACM, Springer, Elsevier, and arXiv.

Evolution and Methodologies of Hybrid Summarization

The development of hybrid summarization techniques has evolved signifcantly, moving from simpler combinations of extractive and shallow abstractive methods to sophisticated deep learning and Large Language Model (LLM)-enhanced architectures. This evolution refects a continuous efort to balance the factual precision of extraction with the linguistic fluency of abstraction.

2.1 Early Hybrid Approaches: Extractive to Shallow Abstractive (E2SA)

Early hybrid models, often termed Extractive-to-Shallow Abstractive (E2SA), primarily focused on refining extracted content with minimal generative modifications. These methods typically involved an initial extractive step to identify key sentences or phrases, followed by light abstractive strategies such as information compression, sentence fusion, or synonym replacement. For instance, some approaches generated extractive summaries based on semantic and statistical features, which were then transformed into abstractive forms using lexical resources such as WordNet, the Lesk algorithm, and part-of-speech taggers [1]. While these methods offered improvements in coherence compared to purely extractive summaries, their abstractive capabilities were limited, often producing summaries that still closely resembled the original text structure. The main limitation of E2SA approaches was their inability to generate truly novel sentences or significantly rephrase content, thereby falling short of human-like summarization.

2.2 Extractive to Abstractive (E2A) Models

As natural language processing advanced, more sophisticated Extractive-to-Abstractive (E2A) methods emerged. These models employ a two-stage process in which an extractive component first selects important sentences, which are then fed into a more robust abstractive summarization model for comprehensive rewriting. A notable example is the EA-LTS model, which uses a hybrid sentence similarity measure for extraction and an RNN-based encoder–decoder with pointer and attention mechanisms for abstraction [1]. This architecture was designed to improve efficiency and accuracy, particularly in long-text summarization, by providing the abstractive component with a condensed and relevant input. The E2A paradigm represented a significant step toward generating more fluent and coherent summaries, as it allowed the abstractive module greater freedom in rephrasing and synthesizing information—moving beyond mere lexical substitutions.

2.3 Deep Learning and Transformer-based Hybrid Models

The advent of deep learning, particularly Transformer architecture, has revolutionized text summarization. Models such as BERT and its variants have been effectively integrated into hybrid systems to enhance both extractive and abstract capabilities. Divya et al. (2024) proposed a unified extractive–abstractive summarization approach that leverages BERT and Transformer models [2]. Their method generates an efficient intermediate representation for each sentence using embedding techniques to capture semantic features. A context-aware extractive system is then developed, learning the document’s context end-to-end to identify informative sentences and eliminate redundancy. Subsequently, an abstract summarizer processes these informative sentences to produce coherent, human-like summaries.

This integration has demonstrated improvements in coverage, relevance, and diversity, while addressing common challenges such as poor readability and redundancy in purely abstractive methods, as well as limited coverage in purely extractive approaches.

2.4 LLM-Enhanced Hybrid Approaches

More recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) have been increasingly utilized to further enhance hybrid summarization. With their advanced generative capabilities, LLMs offer unprecedented flexibility in adapting between extractive, abstractive, and hybrid summarization techniques without requiring extensive retraining [1]. Uçkan (2025) introduced a hybrid model that combines graph entropy with LLMs to improve both summarization accuracy and time efficiency [3]. In this approach, the text is represented as a graph, with sentences as nodes. Karci Entropy (KE) is applied to measure sentence informativeness and select the most valuable ones. These pre-selected sentences are then processed by LLMs (such as BERT, RoBERTa, and XLNet) to generate summaries. This pre-selection significantly reduces the input size for LLMs, resulting in up to a 10% improvement in summary accuracy and a 70–80% reduction in processing time on datasets such as DUC2002 and CNN/Daily Mail. This method effectively addresses the computational cost and efficiency challenges commonly associated with deploying large LLMs for summarization.

Hybrid approaches are also being tailored for specific applications. Krasitskii et al. (2025) proposed a hybrid extractive–abstractive summarization method for multilingual sentiment analysis [4]. Their model integrates TF-IDF-based extraction with a fine-tuned XLM-R abstractive module, enhanced by dynamic thresholding and cultural adaptation. This approach demonstrated significant improvements over baselines across 10 languages, achieving an accuracy of 0.90 for English and 0.84 for low-resource languages, along with a 22% gain in computational efficiency. These findings highlight both the versatility of hybrid methods in addressing complex NLP tasks and their potential for optimization in specialized domains.

Critical Analysis: Strengths, Limitations, and Comparisons

Hybrid extractive-abstractive summarization methods represent a signifcant advancement in ATS, aiming to harness the best of both worlds. A critical examination reveals their inherent strengths, persistent limitations, and how they compare against purely extractive or abstractive paradigms.

3.1 Strengths of Hybrid Approaches

The primary strength of hybrid models lies in their ability to combine the factual accuracy and grounded ness of extractive summarization with the linguistic fluency and coherence of abstractive summarization. By first identifying and extracting key information, hybrid systems ensure that the generated summaries remain faithful to the source content, thereby mitigating the risk of factual inaccuracies and hallucinations often observed in purely abstractive models. The subsequent abstractive component then refines and synthesizes this extracted information, producing summaries that are more readable, grammatically correct, and human-like in their expression [1].

Moreover, hybrid approaches frequently demonstrate superior performance compared to standalone methods, particularly in terms of ROUGE scores (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation), which measure the overlap between generated and reference summaries. For example, Divya et al. (2024) showed that their BERT- and Transformer-based hybrid model achieved improved coverage, relevance, and diversity, outperforming standard summarization techniques on datasets such as CNN/DailyMail and DUC2004 [2]. This performance boost is often attributed to multi-stage processing, where the extractive phase acts as a high-quality filter, providing a condensed input for the abstractive generator. Such pre-processing not only improves accuracy but also enhances efficiency. For instance, Uçkan (2025) demonstrated that a graph entropy–based pre-selection approach reduced the input size for LLMs, leading to up to an 80% reduction in processing time while simultaneously improving accuracy by up to 10% [3]. These efficiency gains are crucial for deploying summarization systems in real-world applications, particularly when handling large volumes of text or resource-intensive LLMs.

3.2 Limitations and Challenges

Despite their advantages, hybrid summarization methods are not without limitations. One persistent challenge arises from the inherent sparsity of extracted summaries. If the extractive component fails to capture all critical information or selects redundant sentences, the subsequent abstractive component may struggle to generate a high-quality, comprehensive summary. In such cases, the outputs can be inferior to those produced by highly optimized purely abstractive approaches, particularly when the latter are trained on large-scale datasets and fine-tuned for fluency [1].

Multilingual summarization presents another significant hurdle. As highlighted by Almohaimeed and Azmi (2025), linguistic diversity, limited resources, and complex language structures pose considerable challenges [5]. Issues such as morphological richness, syntactic variation, semantic divergence across languages, and the scarcity of annotated datasets for many low-resource languages often result in models developed for high-resource languages, such as English, underperforming elsewhere. While some hybrid models, such as that of Krasitskii et al. (2025) for multilingual sentiment analysis, show promise in addressing these issues, generalization across a wide range of languages remains an active area of research [4].

Furthermore, hybrid models—particularly those incorporating abstractive components—remain susceptible to challenges prevalent in generative AI, including factual inaccuracies, incoherent outputs, content hallucinations, and biases. These issues are further compounded by the limitations of current evaluation metrics, which often fail to adequately capture semantic correctness or contextual appropriateness. Ongoing research efforts focus on integrating external world knowledge and developing ethically grounded frameworks to mitigate these shortcomings [5].

3.3 Comparative Analysis of Approaches

Comparing hybrid approaches with their standalone counterparts reveals a nuanced landscape. Purely extractive methods are effective in maintaining factual consistency and are computationally less demanding, but they often generate summaries that lack fluency and may include redundant information. Purely abstractive methods, particularly those powered by advanced LLMs, excel at producing highly fluent and concise summaries that closely resemble human writing. However, they are prone to factual errors and require substantial computational resources as well as large datasets for training.

Hybrid methods aim to strike a balance by combining factual accuracy with linguistic quality. Early E2SA models improved readability compared to purely extractive methods but remained limited in their generative capacity. The progression to E2A models, followed by Transformer- and LLM-enhanced hybrid systems, has progressively narrowed the gap between human- and machine-generated summaries. The primary trade-off lies between model complexity, computational cost, and the desired balance of accuracy and fluency. For example, while LLM-enhanced hybrid models offer superior performance, their deployment necessitates careful consideration of computational demands and efficiency strategies such as pre-selection [3]. Ultimately, the choice of approach depends on the specific application’s requirements—whether prioritizing factual accuracy, high fluency, or efficient use of computational resources.

4. Research Gaps and Future Directions

Despite significant advancements in hybrid extractive–abstractive summarization, several research gaps and promising future directions remain. Addressing these areas will be crucial for developing more robust, efficient, and universally applicable ATS systems.

One prominent gap is the lack of comprehensive evaluation on real-world, noisy datasets. Much of the current research relies on clean, well-structured datasets, which may not accurately reflect the complexities and imperfections of real-world text. Evaluating hybrid models on diverse and noisy datasets—containing grammatical errors, informal language, or domain-specific jargon—is essential to assess their robustness and generalizability. Such evaluations would provide a more realistic understanding of system performance and highlight areas for improvement in practical applications.


Another critical gap lies in the limited focus on low-resource languages. While some progress has been made, such as the multilingual sentiment analysis work by Krasitskii et al. (2025) [4], most advanced hybrid summarization research continues to concentrate on high-resource languages, particularly English. Challenges such as linguistic diversity, morphological richness, and the scarcity of annotated datasets hinder the development and deployment of effective solutions globally. Future research should prioritize strategies for low-resource settings, including cross-lingual transfer learning, data-efficient training, and unsupervised or semi-supervised methods.

The explainability of hybrid models also remains underexplored. As models grow more complex, especially with the integration of deep learning and LLMs, understanding how summaries are generated and how extractive and abstractive components interact is increasingly difficult. Few attempts have been made to provide transparent insights into these decision-making processes. Improving explainability would not only foster trust but also facilitate debugging, refinement, and responsible deployment—particularly in sensitive domains such as healthcare or legal analysis.

Building on the work of Krasitskii et al. (2025), optimization for low-resource languages through techniques like 8-bit quantization represents another promising direction [4]. Such methods can reduce computational demands, making advanced hybrid models more feasible for languages with limited infrastructure or resources. Further exploration of efficient model architectures and compression techniques tailored for these contexts is warranted.

Additionally, the persistence of factual inaccuracies and content hallucinations in abstractive components, as noted by Almohaimeed and Azmi (2025) [5], highlights the need for integrating external world knowledge. Approaches such as incorporating knowledge graphs, factual databases, or external validation mechanisms could help ground abstractive generation, ensuring higher factual consistency. Novel architectures that query external knowledge during generation, or post-processing mechanisms that validate content, represent fruitful avenues for exploration.

Finally, the development of ethically grounded frameworks for hybrid summarization is an emerging but underdeveloped trend [5]. This includes addressing biases in training data, ensuring fairness across demographics and viewpoints, and establishing guidelines for responsible deployment. As ATS systems become increasingly pervasive, mitigating ethical risks will be paramount for their sustainable and trustworthy adoption.

5. Conclusion

Automatic Text Summarization (ATS) has evolved significantly, with hybrid extractive–abstractive methods emerging as a powerful paradigm to overcome the limitations of standalone approaches. By strategically combining the factual accuracy of extractive techniques with the linguistic fluency of abstractive generation, these hybrid models offer a balanced solution for condensing vast amounts of information. The progression from early Extractive-to-Shallow Abstractive (E2SA) methods to sophisticated deep learning– and Large Language Model (LLM)–enhanced architectures reflects a continuous pursuit of more coherent, relevant, and diverse summaries.

Recent advancements, particularly the integration of Transformer models and LLMs, have demonstrated remarkable improvements in performance, efficiency, and applicability across diverse domains. For example, the unified approach by Divya et al. (2024) leverages BERT and Transformer architectures to achieve enhanced coverage and diversity [2]. Similarly, innovations such as Uçkan’s (2025) graph entropy–based pre-selection for LLMs highlight how computational efficiency can be significantly improved while maintaining high accuracy [3]. The adaptability of hybrid methods also extends to specialized applications, as illustrated by Krasitskii et al.’s (2025) work on multilingual sentiment analysis, which successfully addresses language-specific challenges [4].

Despite these successes, the field continues to face pressing challenges, including the evaluation of models on real-world noisy datasets, the equitable development of methods for low-resource languages, and the need for greater explainability in increasingly complex architectures. Future research should focus on bridging these gaps by exploring advanced optimization techniques such as 8-bit quantization for resource-constrained settings, integrating external world knowledge to mitigate factual inaccuracies, and developing ethically grounded frameworks to ensure fairness and accountability.




As the volume of digital information continues to expand, hybrid extractive–abstractive summarization methods will play an increasingly vital role in making information more accessible and digestible, thereby driving innovation and progress in the broader field of Natural Language Processing.
